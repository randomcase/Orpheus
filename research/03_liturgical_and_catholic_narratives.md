# Liturgical Retellings & Catholic Narratives of Orpheus

## 1. The Early Christian Bridge: *Christus-Orpheus* in the Roman Catacombs

When early Christians painted underground burial chambers (*cubicula*) in Rome during the 3rd and 4th centuries CE—most famously in the **Catacombs of Domitilla**, **Saint Callixtus**, and **Santi Pietro e Marcellino**—one unexpected classical figure appeared repeatedly right beside Daniel in the lions' den and Jonah emerging from the fish: **Orpheus playing his lyre**.

* **Why Use Orpheus in Christian Funerary Art?** Orpheus did not carry violent military baggage like Mars or imperial conquest monuments. He was already culturally legible as the peaceful singer whose music charmed wild beasts and descended into Hades to conquer death.
* **Iconographic Synthesis:** Catacomb frescoes place Orpheus at the center of concentric medallions surrounded by sheep, peacocks (ancient emblems of incorruptibility), and lions—visually fusing **Orpheus Charming the Beasts** with **Jesus the Good Shepherd** and **Lord of the Harrowing of Hell**.

## 2. Patristic Theology: Clement & Eusebius

| Father of the Church | Work & Date | Stance Toward Orpheus | Theological Argument |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Clement of Alexandria** | *Protrepticus* Ch. 1 (c. 195 CE) | Polemical contrast & fulfillment | Orpheus only enchanted wild beasts into temporary stillness and bound mortals to idols; **Christ the Logos** is the "New Song" who transforms savage human hearts into peaceful children of God |
| **Eusebius of Caesarea** | *Tricennial Oration* 14.5 (c. 336 CE) | Positive musical analogy | Just as Orpheus tuned his lyre to calm chaos, the **Incarnate Word** took human flesh as an instrument (*organon*) to reconcile heaven, earth, and nations into one cosmic liturgy |
| **St. Augustine** | *City of God* XVIII.14, 37 | Historical caution | Respects Orpheus as an ancient poet-theologian among the Gentiles, while firmly separating pagan prophecy from biblical revelation |

## 3. Boethius & Medieval Catholic Moral Exegesis

### A. Boethius (*Consolation of Philosophy*, 524 CE)
Writing in prison in Pavia before his execution, the Roman Catholic statesman **Boethius** fused Orpheus's backward glance with Gospel asceticism (*Luke 9:62*: *"No one who puts a hand to the plow and looks back is fit for the kingdom of God"*):
* **Upper Daylight:** Contemplation of God, the Supreme Good (*Summum Bonum*).
* **Tartarus:** Earthly attachment, darkness, and temporal illusion.
* **Looking Back:** The soul that climbs toward grace yet glances back toward vanished earthly idols loses the very salvation it almost held.

### B. *Ovide Moralisé* & Pierre Bersuire (*c. 1317–1340 CE*)
During the High Middle Ages, Catholic friars refused to discard Ovid; instead, they applied the **fourfold sense of Scripture** (*literal, allegorical, tropological/moral, anagogical*) to *Metamorphoses* Book X:
* **Orpheus** = Christ the Bridegroom descending in the **Harrowing of Hell** (*Descensus ad Inferos*, confessed in the Apostles' Creed).
* **Eurydice** = Eve / fallen human nature bitten by the **Serpent** in the meadow of Eden.
* **The Lyre** = The Cross strung with Christ's flesh, sounding harmony between Old and New Testaments.
* **Crucial Theological Contrast:** Unlike pagan Orpheus, **Christ does not look back in weakness**—he breaks the bronze gates and raises humanity permanently on Holy Saturday.

### C. *Sir Orfeo* (c. 1330 CE): Catholic Penitential Romance
In the Middle English *Sir Orfeo*, the backward-glance tragedy is intentionally overwritten. King Orfeo abdicates his throne in humility, wanders ten years barefoot in wilderness penance like a hermit-saint, wins Queen Heurodis back through pure fidelity and music, and restores a righteous kingdom—converting classical fatalism into medieval Christian hope.
